Race plans
Three things that matter more than any workout
  • Easy means easy. If easy days turn medium-hard, the hard days stop working and you're just tired all the time.
  • Take the day off. At least one full rest day a week. You're still growing — that day is when you get faster.
  • Eat and sleep. Nine hours of sleep and enough food beat every session in this app.
Where this plan comes from
Sandford, Kilding, Ross & Laursen (2019), Sports Medicine 49:843–852. In elite male 800 m runners, maximum sprint speed and the anaerobic speed reserve — the gap between top sprint speed and aerobic speed — separate the very best, and 800 runners split into speed types, hybrids and endurance types. This is why you are called a speed type, and why the plan never lets your sprint speed go.
Sandford & Stellingwerff (2019), "Question Your Categories." Runners with almost identical 800 times can have completely different physiologies, so training should be built around the individual rather than a template. This is why the plan runs off your numbers, not a generic 800 program.
Haugen, Sandbakk, Enoksen, Seiler & Tønnessen (2021), "Crossing the Golden Training Divide," Sports Medicine 51:1835–1854. The main review of how world-class 800 and 1500 m runners actually train. 800 specialists do considerably more speed, strength, power and anaerobic work than 1500 runners while still doing a large amount of aerobic running. This is the shape of your whole week.
Haugen, Sandbakk, Seiler & Tønnessen (2022), Sports Medicine Open 8:46. Elite distance runners do roughly 80% or more of their total running volume at low intensity. This is why so many of your days are easy runs.
Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes (2018), Sports Medicine 48:1117–1149, plus Blagrove et al. (2018), Med Sci Sports Exerc 50:1224–1232 on post-puberty teenage distance runners specifically. Two to three strength sessions a week benefit middle- and long-distance runners. This is why lifting is in the plan and is not optional.
Llanos-Lagos et al. (2024), Sports Medicine. Heavy strength work and mixed strength methods improved running economy in middle- and long-distance runners; light-load and isometric-only work did much less. This is why gym days use real load and low reps instead of endless high-rep circuits.
Spencer & Gastin (2001). Energy system contribution by race: roughly 43% aerobic for the 400, 66% for the 800 and 84% for the 1500. This is why building the engine is the fastest route to a better 800 — the 800 is already mostly aerobic.
Youth Running Consensus Statement (2021), British Journal of Sports Medicine. Guidelines for young runners: at least one rest day per week, planned breaks across the year, enough food, and training built around growth rather than copied from adults. This is why there is a day off, and why nobody here is copying elite mileage or Norwegian double-threshold training.
Coaching practice. The overall build — big aerobic base first, race-specific work last — is classic Lydiard and Peter Coe periodisation. The short hill sprints and strides that protect sprint speed all year follow Charlie Francis and the ALTIS group (Dan Pfaff, Stuart McMillan, Danny Mackey), whose Coaching the Endurance Events curriculum covers the 800 upward.
Charlie Francis, ALTIS (Pfaff, McMillan) and Tony Holler. Hard days and easy days kept apart, sprints done fresh with full rest, and the times written down. Speed endurance and special endurance for the 400 (fast 150s, 200s, 300 + 100s) use very few reps with very long rest. This is why hard days never land back to back, and why the rest between fast reps is so long.

A research summary is a starting point, not a prescription for one person. Your coach watches you run. This app doesn't.
